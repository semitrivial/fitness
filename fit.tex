\begin{abstract}
    We introduce a new methodology for measuring evolutionary fitness
    of sets of organisms.
    This methodology is based on the so-called identical ancestor point
    axiom, introduced in 2026 in the Journal of Mathematical Biology,
    which says that in any species, any organism is either the ancestor
    of almost all the future species (we here call such organisms ``winners'')
    or else an ancestor of almost none of the future species (we here
    call such organisms ``losers''). For any set of organisms in a species,
    we simply take the proportion of winners in that set to be a measure
    of fitness. We demonstrate this new method by reporting fitness measurements
    for simulations run in silica.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The idea that evolutionary fitness is synonymous with having more children
is almost a cliche because it is invoked so often.
But this naive approach does not do full justice to the nuances of fitness.
If I give birth to a million sterile children (and no fertile children),
then that should not make me fit whatsoever. Giving birth to one fertile child
should confer more fitness than giving birth to any number of sterile children.

We could attempt to patch the above problem by considering grandchildren
rather than children: measure evolutionary fitness by how many grandchildren
an organism has. But this just pushes the bubble around under the wallpaper,
because one could theoretically have numerous sterile grandchildren (and no
fertile grandchildren), which would also make one manifestly unfit. Delaying
the calculation any number of generations runs into the same problem.

To be clear, the problem does not merely arise in situations like ``all of this
organism's children are sterile,'' which situations may seem contrived.
The problem can arise in very realistic scenarios. For example, as noted by
\cite{alexander2021can},
organisms could inhabit an environment which very rarely suffers from heatwaves.
If the heatwaves are rare enough, organisms could evolve to lose expensive heatwave
resistance, which would seem (in the short term) to make them more fit.
But then if all such mutants are killed by the next heatwave, then the
mutation was actually unfit.

Another example: naive ``fitness $=$ number of children'' considerations
often gloss over
the fact that in nature, organisms can sexually reproduce with their own children.
It is not clear whether co-parenting offspring
with its own child should grant the same
amount of fitness to a fruitfly as co-parenting offspring
with an unrelated fruitfly mate.

We address this by using the idea of identical ancestor points from
\cite{chang1999recent} and \cite{rohde2004modelling},
more recently studied by \cite{alexander2026specieslike}.
Motivated by the idea of identical
ancestor points, we consider two types of organisms. We call an organism $x$ a
\emph{winner} if, at some point in time (probably in the distant future after
$x$'s death), all living members of $x$'s species are descended from $x$.
We call $x$ a \emph{loser} if $x$'s descendants eventually go extinct.
We argued \cite{alexander2026specieslike} for what we call
the \emph{identical ancestor point axiom}.
This axiom states that in any species, every organism of that species is either
a winner or a loser, in the above sense. This offers a shockingly simple
new way to measure the fitness of a finite set $S$ of organisms in a
species, assuming the species satisfies the axiom. Namely: the fitness of
$S$ is
\[
    \frac{\mbox{number of winners in $S$}}{|S|}.
\]

In this paper we discuss empirical results about such fitness based on
simulations \emph{in silica}.

The roadmap of this paper is as follows:

    \item
    In Section \ref{summarysecn}, we summarize and make concluding remarks.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theory behind this new methodology}
\label{theoryscn}


The so-called \emph{identical ancestor point axiom} states that any species can be
divided into
so-called \emph{winners} and \emph{losers}, defined in the
Introduction. This is not useful for studying the fitness of any particular
organism, because any particular organism is either a winner or a loser within
its species, and so would be given fitness value of either $0$ or $1$, which is
too granular to be of any use. Thus, we instead measure the fitness of sets of
organisms in a species.

This winner/loser notion
is crucial for our methodology, but at first glance,
it seems only useful for idealized species
that don't go extinct. For, if a species goes extinct, then all its
members are winners \emph{and} losers\footnote{Losers because their
descendants in the species go extinct; winners because after the
species has gone extinct, vacuously all living members are their
descendants. For example, if the human species goes extinct in the
year 3000 then we can truthfully say that in the year 3001, every
living human is this author's descendant---it is vacuously true
since there are no living humans in 3001.}. However,
as the empirical evidence below demonstrates, the methodology can
effectively be applied to more realistic species if we have data
over a time interval long relative to the time
required for mixing, but short enough to avoid extinction.
In that case, we can simply pretend that the species continues forever
beyond our data set.

Sets of organisms correspond naturally to actions, in the sense that
to any action, we can associate the set of organisms who take that action.
For example, consider the act of eating a poisonous mushroom.
This action can be associated with the set which contains precisely those
organisms who ate a poisonous mushroom during their lifetime.
Thus, being able to measure fitness of sets of organisms, we can measure
% following lines thanks to dragoon347
fitness of actions (but of course this should really be thought of as
fitness of actions in particular environments).

Absolute values computed using this methodology are, as far as we know
currently, not meaningful by themselves (save only in degenerate cases
such as $0$ or $1$). Rather, what are meaningful are the values relative
to other values. In other words, knowing that the set of organisms who
eat poisonous mushrooms has a fitness score of $0.65$ is not particularly
meaningful in itself, but can be compared to other scores. If the fitness
score of eating delicious strawberries is $0.85$ then since
$0.85>0.65$, we conclude that eating delicious strawberries is more
evolutionarily fit than eating poisonous mushrooms.

% Following paragraph inspired by discussion w/blank-w9t
As a special case, we could consider the fitness of singletons, i.e.,
of individual organisms. However, restricted to individual organisms,
fitness, measured this way, is a boolean variable. If the organism is
a winner then it has fitness $1$, and if it is a loser, then it has
fitness $0$. So the measure is not very useful when applied to singletons.

\section{A python simulation}
\label{simulationsecn}

This paper is intended as a proof of concept for the
aforementioned fitness-measuring methodology.
We implemented a simplisitic simulation of organisms using
python. In this simulation, a small 2-dimensional world is initially populated
with a random number of randomly scattered organisms, all of the same species.
Time in this world is discrete, broken up into \emph{turns}. On every turn,
every organism takes one \emph{action}. Possible actions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attempt to walk north.
    \item Attempt to walk south.
    \item Attempt to walk east.
    \item Attempt to walk west.
    \item Attempt to eat adjacent food.
    \item Attempt to reproduce with adjacent fellow organism.
\end{itemize}
A $Q$-learner reinforcement learning (RL) agent \cite{watkins2026learning}
was used to choose which actions
each organism took on each turn.
Observations were passed to the RL agent as if the organisms
had severely limited eyesight, in order to facilitate quick training of the
agent ($Q$-learners are very quick to act, but the time it takes them to learn
their environment grows exponentially in the eyesight of the agents).
This means the organisms acted purely on instinct with no conscious memories,
choosing each action solely based upon what they saw at that moment and not
directly upon what they had seen earlier (though the reinforcement learning
agent did train to update its weights, so that the organisms did possess a
kind of communal instinctual memory).

Because this paper is only intended as a simple proof of concept, we will not
go into fine details here about the exact parameters used in the simulation;
the interested reader can consult the code itself, available on Github ....
One particular detail which is important for understanding the results we
obtained concerns how reproduction works in this simulation. In our simulation,
when an organism attempts to reproduce with an adjacent fellow organism, the
attempt will fail unless both of the following conditions are true:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item There is \emph{exactly one} other organism adjacent to the organism
        who is attempting to reproduce.
    \item There is at least one empty space adjacent to the organism
        who is attempting to reproduce (so that the child, which is born
        immediately, has a place to be born into).
\end{enumerate}
We implemented the simulation this way in order to simplify many troublesome
questions regarding parenthood and so on, while keeping the available action
list manageable. As a side effect, this causes crowding to naturally reduce
fertility, since, in a large crowd, it is difficult to satisfy condition 1.
We will see in the next section that this apparently resulted in a non-increasing
child-count vs.\ fitness score: beyond a certain point, having more children
was measured to actually confer \emph{less} fitness, probably due to this
crowding mechanism. While this is of course contrived and unnatural, it
nevertheless points to similar phenomena plausible in real life: beyond a certain
point, having too many children really could be detrimental to one's fitness,
if those children are competing for limited resources, etc.

Within the simulation, food periodically grows at random locations in the
environment. This food is split into eight different types (below, Strl should
be read as ``sterile''; Fut should be read as ``future'';
Desc should be read as ``descendant'';
Frt should be read as ``fertility bonus''):
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathsf{Normal}$ food has no effect other than default satiation.
    \item $\mathsf{StrlFutDesc}$ food is a food which has the
        following effect. After eating the food, any time that the eater
        subsequently reproduces, the offspring will be born sterile.
        Said offspring will never succeed at reproducing. Thus, if one eats
        this food before having
        any offspring, then one cannot ever have any grandchildren.
    \item $\mathsf{StrlAllDesc}$ food is a food which causes
        all of the eater's descendants to become sterile, including
        descendants born before or after the food is eaten (of course
        this is physically unrealistic, but we included it because
        it illuminates how our fitness measure works).
    \item $\mathsf{StrlFutDescPlusFrt}$ food is exactly the same
        as $\mathsf{StrlFutDesc}$ food, except that it additionally
        confers an added fertility bonus to the eater, so that the eater
        will enjoy easier reproduction after eating it. (However, the offspring
        of this easier reproduction will be sterile.)
    \item $\mathsf{StrlAllDescFrt}$ food is exactly the same
        as $\mathsf{StrlAllDesc}$ food, except it additionally confers an
        added fertility bonus, as in the previous bullet.
    \item $\mathsf{Frt}$ food is a food which grants additional fertility to
        its eater (in exactly the same way as in the previous two bullets).
        It has no other side effects.
    \item $\mathsf{Insanity}$ food is a food which hijacks the
        reinforcement learning
        agent driving the eater. After eating insanity food, the eater will
        thereafter act in ways which maximize punishment and minimize reward
        (rather than the usual strategy of minimizing punishment and
        maximizing reward). This is partly inspired by \cite{alexander2021reward}.
    \item $\mathsf{Shortsightedness}$ food is a food which alters the observations
        which are sent to the reinforcement learning agent in order to choose
        the eater's actions. To make a long story short, this food has the
        effect of reducing the eater's range of vision.
\end{itemize}

\section{Empirical results from the simulation}
\label{empiricalsecn}

\subsection{Data and estimates}

We ran the simulation $3{,}123$ times, one run per random seed, stopping each
run after $100{,}000$ turns. $393$ runs ($12.6\%$) went extinct, always early:
the mean extinction time was $128$ turns and the latest was turn $262$.
Extinct runs are tiny (on average $18.8$ organisms were ever created in one).
They also cannot be analysed with the rest. When the last organism of a run
dies, every organism in that run satisfies the definition of a winner, and
this produced the only organisms in the whole dataset flagged as both winners
and losers ($6{,}217$). It also produced the only ``winners'' who had eaten
sterilizing food. We therefore report results for the $2{,}730$ runs that
survived all $100{,}000$ turns.

These runs produced $752{,}304{,}437$ organisms. Of these, $749{,}077{,}401$
were resolved as either a winner or a loser by the end of the run, and
$3{,}227{,}036$ ($0.43\%$) were still unresolved. Resolution happens quickly:
every resolved organism in the data was resolved within $209$ turns of its
birth. The median time from birth to resolution was $80$ turns for losers
and $106$ turns for winners. Unresolved organisms were therefore born in the
last few hundred turns of a run, and we exclude them. This exclusion is not
harmless for organisms born near the end, because a late-born organism is only
resolved if it loses quickly (see Section \ref{sec:time-blocks}).

For a set $S$ of resolved organisms, the fitness of $S$ is the proportion of
winners in $S$. For a food type, $S$ is the set of organisms that ate that food
at least once during their lifetime. These sets overlap, since an organism may
eat several food types.

\subsection{Uncertainty}

Organisms within a run share ancestors and an environment, so they are not
independent observations. We therefore computed confidence intervals by
resampling whole runs (a cluster bootstrap with $2{,}000$ resamples). We also
repeated this with clusters defined by run \emph{and} $5{,}000$-turn window of
birth,
%(following a suggestion from blank-w9t),
to allow for correlation among
organisms born around the same time. The clustering turned out to matter very
little. The ratio of the cluster-bootstrap variance to the naive binomial
variance (the \emph{design effect}) was between $0.9$ and $1.2$ for every
food type. Because the sample is so large, the intervals below are narrow. We
stress that a narrow interval only says a difference is real \emph{in this
simulation}; it does not say the difference is large.

\end{itemize}

We draw the following conclusions.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item As expected, every food that permanently sterilizes all of the
    eater's descendants
        has fitness exactly $0$ in surviving runs. This
        illustrates the point we made in the Introduction. Even the extra
        fertility granted by eating
        $\mathsf{StrlAllDescsFrt}$ does not help.
    \item The ranking $\mathsf{Frt} > \mathsf{Normal} > \mathsf{Insanity} >
        \mathsf{StrlFutDescs} > \mathsf{StrlAllDescs}$ is not a fluke of
        pooling many runs together. In particular, $\mathsf{Frt}$ beat
        $\mathsf{Normal}$, and $\mathsf{Normal}$ beat $\mathsf{Insanity}$,
        in every single run.
    \item $\mathsf{Shortsighted}$ food carries a real but very small penalty,
        about a quarter of a percentage point. The penalty is detectable only
        because the dataset is very large. In a single run it goes the
        ``wrong'' way almost half the time.
    \item We cannot distinguish $\mathsf{StrlFutDescs}$ from
        $\mathsf{StrlFutDescsFrt}$. This indicates that the fertility bonus
        does not improve fitness, even though eaters of that food have more
        children. This is consistent with what we said in the Introduction.
\end{enumerate}

For every food, eating ``late'' goes with higher fitness, even for
$\mathsf{Normal}$ food ($0.87$ vs.\ $0.91$). This is because organisms that

two sterilizing-future-descendants foods stand out. Eaten before any

points relative to $\mathsf{Normal}$. The headline score of about $0.48$ is
therefore a mixture of two very different populations. This illustrates
something the methodology captures naturally and a naive offspring count
does not: the fitness of an action depends on the circumstances in which it is
taken.


\subsection{Stability over time}
\label{sec:time-blocks}

The Q-learner was frozen before the runs used here, so the organisms' behaviour
does not change over time within a run. Consistent with this, fitness scores are
flat across the run. In each $5{,}000$-turn window of birth from turn $5{,}000$ to
turn $95{,}000$, $\mathsf{Normal}$ fitness stays between $0.7676$ and $0.7692$,
and the other foods are similarly stable. The two edge windows are the
exception.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Organisms born in turns $0$--$5{,}000$ are slightly less fit
        ($\mathsf{Normal}$: $0.7643$). This presumably reflects a start-up period in
        which the population is still settling down from its random initial
        state.
    \item Organisms born in turns $95{,}000$--$100{,}000$ are noticeably less
        fit ($\mathsf{Normal}$: $0.7519$). This is due to a truncation effect:
        an organism born near the end of a run is only counted if it
        is resolved in time. Losing is quicker than winning, so losers are
        over-represented here.
\end{itemize}
If both edge windows are dropped, every fitness score in
Table \ref{tab:fitness-by-food} moves by at most $0.0015$
(e.g.\ $\mathsf{Normal}$ $0.7682$, $\mathsf{Shortsighted}$ $0.7663$,
$\mathsf{StrlFutDescs}$ $0.4791$, $\mathsf{StrlFutDescsFrt}$ $0.4782$), and the
ranking is unchanged.

\subsection{Fitness by number of children}

We can also compute the fitness of the set of organisms that had exactly $n$
children in their lifetime (Figure \ref{childrencountfigure} and
Table \ref{tab:winner-loser-counts-by-children}). Organisms with no children
have fitness $0$ by definition. Fitness rises steeply from $0.764$ (one child)
to $0.937$ (two) and $0.976$ (three). It peaks at five children ($0.9870$,
95\% interval $[0.9870, 0.9871]$). After that it declines slowly, to $0.9770$
$[0.9750, 0.9789]$ at ten. The decline from five to ten children is well
outside sampling error, but it is only one percentage point. It also affects
few organisms: $1.6\%$ of resolved organisms had six or more children, and
$0.1\%$ had eight or more. Beyond ten children, the intervals are too wide to
say anything about the shape of the curve. The organisms with $14$ or more
children ($169$ in total, only one of them a loser) are pooled in the table
and figure.

We believe the decline is caused by the crowding rule of
Section \ref{simulationsecn}. The number of children is itself
an outcome of an organism's life, not a choice made independently of it, so
Figure \ref{childrencountfigure} shows an association, not a dose-response
relationship. What it does show clearly is that the naive view ``more children
means more fitness'' breaks down beyond about five children. For most
organisms, however, the first two or three children matter far more than any
later ones.

\subsection{Fitness scores can yield unexpected insight into behaviour}

When we programmed the simulation behind these results, we did not expect
that the $\mathsf{Shortsighted}$ food would be so close to $\mathsf{Normal}$ food.
Since eating $\mathsf{Shortsighted}$ food causes an organism to
become short-sighted,
we expected that the fitness of eating such food would be much more significantly
reduced. But the experimental evidence shows this is not the case.
Of course this does not tell us anything about the pros or cons of being
short-sighted \emph{in general}, but only in this particular type of simulation:
it shows us that in this particular type of simulation, agents driven by this
particular RL agent, do not benefit from extended vision.
This
illustrates one benefit of calculating fitness scores: it can give us unexpected
insight into an environment in various ways. We speculate that if it were
possible to calculate fitness scores for actions that we humans perform,
some of the scores would probably be quite surprising.

\section{Summary and conclusion}
\label{summarysecn}

We introduced a new methodology for measuring evolutionary fitness of
sets of organisms. This serves as a proxy for measuring evolutionary
fitness of \emph{actions}, namely: the fitness of an action can be
thought of as the fitness of the set of organisms who take said action.

This methodology is based on the idea of dividing the species into
so-called \emph{winners} and \emph{losers}. A \emph{winner} is an
organism who is ancestor to all but finitely many future members of the species.
A \emph{loser} is an organism whose descendants eventually go extinct.
The \emph{fitness} of a set of organisms is simply the proportion of winners
in that set.
This methodology hinges on the theory introduced
in \cite{alexander2026specieslike}, where we argued
that it is plausible that species ought to be defined in such a way that
the \emph{identical ancestor point axiom} holds, which axiom is the statement
that every organism in any species is either a winner or a loser.
This is what makes the methodology make sense (a priori, if not all organisms
were winners or losers, then the non-winner-non-loser organisms would complicate
things).

We ran simulations to demonstrate this new methodology \emph{in silica}.
The results agree with intuition, with one exception, namely that we found
no fitness penalty associated with eating a certain food which causes the
eater to become shortsighted. We speculate that this reveals that shortsightedness
is not particularly detrimental in the specific worlds we simulated:
showing that these fitness results can reveal things to us which were
not clear beforehand (we had no idea beforehand that, the way the worlds
we simulated work, shortsightedness was so harmless there).

It would be very interesting to experimentally measure fitness of actions
in this way with a living, real-world population. Of course, this would be
significantly more difficult, not only because it would require much more time
for the population to proceed, but also because it would require very careful
